2. Utility

Representation, ordinality, transformations, and continuous representations.

2.1 Utility Representation

Let $X$ be the set of alternatives.

A utility function $u: X \to \mathbb{R}$ represents a preference relation $\succeq$ if:

$\boxed{x \succeq y \iff u(x) \ge u(y)}$
A utility function assigns numbers to alternatives in a way that preserves their ranking.
  • Utility represents preferences — it does not create them.
  • Utility representations are generally not unique.
Ordinal vs. Cardinal
Utility is ordinal, not cardinal. The numerical values themselves do not measure the intensity of preference.

Suppose $u(A) - u(B) = 5$  and  $u(A) - u(C) = 1$.

This does not imply that the preference for $A$ over $B$ is 5 times stronger than the preference for $A$ over $C$. Only the order matters ($u(A) > u(B) > u(C)$), not the differences between the numbers.

2.2 Representation and Rationality
$\boxed{\text{If } \succeq \text{ is representable by a utility function, then } \succeq \text{ is rational.}}$
Rational means complete and transitive.

Real numbers can always be compared ($\ge$) and their ordering is transitive, so a preference relation represented by real-valued utility inherits completeness and transitivity.

Part 1: Completeness

Since $u(x), u(y) \in \mathbb{R}$, either $u(x) \ge u(y)$ or $u(y) \ge u(x)$.

Therefore, $x \succeq y$ or $y \succeq x$. Hence $\succeq$ is complete.

Part 2: Transitivity

Suppose $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$.

Then $u(x) \ge u(y)$ and $u(y) \ge u(z)$, which implies $u(x) \ge u(z)$.

Therefore, $x \succeq z$. Hence $\succeq$ is transitive. $\blacksquare$

2.3 Does Rationality Imply Utility Representation?
$\boxed{\text{Representable} \Rightarrow \text{Rational}} \quad \text{(Always)}$

The reverse implication depends on the choice set $X$:

Finite Choice Set ($X$ is finite)
$\boxed{\text{Rational} \Rightarrow \text{Representable}}$
With finitely many alternatives, a complete and transitive ranking can always be encoded by real numbers.

If $a \succ b \succ c$, we can simply assign:

$u(a) = 3, \quad u(b) = 2, \quad u(c) = 1$

This preserves $a \succ b \succ c$ perfectly.

Infinite Choice Set ($X$ is infinite)
$\boxed{\text{Rational} \not\Rightarrow \text{Representable in general}}$
"Not necessarily" does not mean infinite choice sets are never representable. Some rational preferences on infinite sets ARE representable.

On $X = \mathbb{R}_+$, define $x \succeq y \iff x \ge y$.

This rational preference relation on an infinite set is easily represented by:

$u(x) = x$
2.4 Increasing Transformations
Proposition
If $u$ represents $\succeq$, then for any strictly increasing function $v: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, the composition $v \circ u$ also represents $\succeq$.
$u(x) \ge u(y) \iff v(u(x)) \ge v(u(y))$
Strictly increasing transformations preserve rankings. Therefore, utility is ordinal.

By definition of utility representation:

$x \succeq y \iff u(x) \ge u(y)$

Since $v$ is strictly increasing:

$u(x) \ge u(y) \iff v(u(x)) \ge v(u(y))$

Therefore: $x \succeq y \iff v(u(x)) \ge v(u(y))$, so $v \circ u$ also represents $\succeq$. $\blacksquare$

Let $u(A)=3, u(B)=2, u(C)=1$, representing $A \succ B \succ C$.

Apply the strictly increasing transformation $v(t) = 10t + 100$:

$v(u(A)) = 130, \quad v(u(B)) = 120, \quad v(u(C)) = 110$

Since $130 > 120 > 110$, the preference ranking $A \succ B \succ C$ is completely unchanged.

2.5 Continuity and Utility Representation
$\boxed{\text{When } X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n, \text{ Rational } + \text{ Continuous Preferences } \Rightarrow \text{ Continuous Utility Representation } u: X \to \mathbb{R}}$
Continuity prevents "jumps" in preferences. If a sequence of bundles $y^k \to y$ approaches $y$, then preferences behave consistently in the limit.

Lexicographic Preferences on $\mathbb{R}^2_+$ are defined by: $x \succ y$ if either $x_1 > y_1$, or ($x_1 = y_1$ and $x_2 > y_2$).

The first coordinate has absolute priority; the second coordinate matters only if the first coordinates are equal.

Counterexample Proof of Non-Continuity:

Let $x = (0.5, 0.5)$ and $y^k = \left(0.5 + \frac{1}{k}, 0\right)$.

  • For every finite $k$: $y^k \succ x$  because $0.5 + \frac{1}{k} > 0.5$.
  • In the limit as $k \to \infty$: $y^k \to (0.5, 0)$.
  • Direct comparison gives: $x \succ (0.5, 0)$  because first coordinates are equal ($0.5 = 0.5$) and $0.5 > 0$.

Preferences "jump" at the limit: $y^k \succ x$ for all $k$, but $\lim y^k \prec x$.

Conclusion: Lexicographic preferences are rational but not continuous, and therefore cannot be represented by a continuous utility function.
