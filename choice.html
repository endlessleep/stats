Part I — Choice Theory

3. Choices

Revealed preferences, rationalizability, Property α, and Houthakker’s Axiom (HARP).

Let $X$ be the set of possible alternatives.
A non-empty subset $A \subseteq X$ from which an agent can choose is called a choice set (or menu).
Part 1 · Single-Valued
Choice Function $C_F$
Selects exactly one alternative:
$$C_F(A) \in A \quad \text{e.g. } C(\{x, y, z\}) = x$$
$$\boxed{C \text{ rationalizable } \iff \text{Property } \alpha}$$
Part 2 · Multi-Valued
Choice Rule $C_R$
Selects a set of maximal alternatives (allows ties $\sim$):
$$C_R(A) \subseteq A \quad \text{e.g. } C(\{x, y, z\}) = \{x, y\}$$
$$\boxed{C \text{ rationalizable } \iff \text{HARP}}$$
PART 1 · SINGLE-VALUED CHOICE
1.1 Choice Functions & Rationalizability

A choice function $C_F$ assigns to every choice set $A$ exactly one chosen alternative:

$$\boxed{C_F(A) \in A}$$

A choice function $C$ is rationalizable if there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ on $X$ such that, for every choice set $A$, $C(A)$ is the unique strictly best alternative in $A$:

$$\boxed{C(A) \succ y \qquad \forall y \in A, \ y \neq C(A)}$$
Rational
A property of underlying preferences: completeness + transitivity.
Rationalizable
A property of observed choices: they can be generated by rational preferences.

Let $X = \{x, y, h\}$ (e.g. going out with Xavier, with Yoan, or staying home):

$$C(\{x, h\}) = x, \qquad C(\{y, h\}) = y, \qquad C(\{x, y, h\}) = h$$

From $C(\{x, h\}) = x$, we infer $x \succ h$.
However, when $y$ is added to the menu, $h$ is chosen ($C(\{x, y, h\}) = h$) even though $x$ is still available.

Therefore, this choice function is not rationalizable.

1.2 Characterization: Property α
$$\boxed{\text{A choice function } C \text{ is rationalizable } \iff C \text{ satisfies Property } \alpha}$$
Definition — Property α
For any $B \subset A \subseteq X$, if $C(A) \in B$, then:
$$\boxed{C(B) = C(A)}$$
If the old winner is still available after some unchosen alternatives are removed, it must remain the winner.
Direction 1: Rationalizable $\implies$ Property α
Starting point: Suppose $C$ is rationalizable.
Then, by definition, there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ such that, for every choice set $A$, $C(A)$ is the unique strictly best alternative in $A$.

Since $C(A)$ is the unique best alternative in $A$:

$$C(A) \succ y \qquad \forall y \in A, \ y \neq C(A)$$

Assume $B \subset A$ and $C(A) \in B$.

Since $B \subset A$, it follows directly that:

$$C(A) \succ y \qquad \forall y \in B, \ y \neq C(A)$$

Hence $C(A)$ is also the unique strictly best alternative in $B$.

Therefore: $C(B) = C(A)$. Thus $C$ satisfies Property $\alpha$. $\blacksquare$

Direction 2: Property α $\implies$ Rationalizable
Starting point: Suppose $C$ satisfies Property $\alpha$.
Now we do not yet have a preference relation: we construct one from pairwise choices (e.g. $x \succeq y \iff C(\{x, y\}) = x$) and prove that Property $\alpha$ guarantees completeness, transitivity, and reproduction of observed choices.

PART 2 · MULTI-VALUED CHOICE (CHOICE RULES)
2.1 Choice Rules (Correspondences) & Rationalizability

A choice function assumes a single winner. When an agent is indifferent between multiple items, several alternatives can be equally optimal. A choice rule (or choice correspondence) $C_R$ selects a non-empty subset of alternatives:

$$\boxed{C_R(A) \subseteq A \qquad \text{and} \qquad C_R(A) \neq \varnothing}$$

A choice rule $C$ is rationalizable if there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ such that $C(A)$ corresponds to the set of maximal alternatives in $A$:

$$\boxed{C(A) = \{x \in A : x \succeq y \quad \forall y \in A\}}$$
  • $C(A)$ is the set of all elements in $A$ that are at least as good as every other element in $A$.
  • Multiple alternatives belong to $C(A)$ simultaneously whenever they are indifferent ($x \sim y$).
2.2 Houthakker’s Axiom of Revealed Preferences (HARP)

For multi-valued choice rules, the consistency axiom is HARP. For all $a, b \in A \cap B$ such that $a \in C(A)$ and $b \in C(B)$:

$$\boxed{a \in C(B) \quad \text{and} \quad b \in C(A)}$$
Intuition:
— If $a$ is chosen when $b$ is available ($a \in C(A), b \in A$), this reveals $a \succeq b$.
— If $b$ is chosen when $a$ is available ($b \in C(B), a \in B$), this reveals $b \succeq a$.
Therefore $a \sim b$. Since both are tied at the top, both must be maximal in both choice sets!

Note: HARP does not mean $a = b$. It means choice behavior across menus is free of contradictory preference reversals.

Consider $A = \{a, b, c\}$ with $C(A) = \{a\}$, and $B = \{a, b\}$ with $C(B) = \{b\}$.

  • $a, b \in A \cap B$.
  • $a \in C(A)$ reveals $a \succeq b$.
  • $b \in C(B)$ reveals $b \succeq a$.

HARP requires $a \in C(B)$ and $b \in C(A)$. But here $a \notin C(B)$ and $b \notin C(A)$.

Conclusion: The choice rule violates HARP and cannot be rationalized.

2.3 Main Characterization Theorem (Finite $X$)
$$\boxed{\text{Given a finite set } X, \text{ a choice rule } C \text{ is rationalizable } \iff C \text{ satisfies HARP.}}$$
Direction 1: Rationalizable $\implies$ HARP
Starting point: Suppose $C$ is rationalizable.
Then, by definition, there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ such that $C(A)$ contains the maximal alternatives of every choice set $A$.

Consider $a, b \in A \cap B$ such that $a \in C(A)$ and $b \in C(B)$.

  • By maximality Since $a \in C(A)$ and $b \in A$, we have: $a \succeq b$.
  • By maximality Since $b \in C(B)$ and $a \in B$, we have: $b \succeq a$.

Hence: $a \sim b$.

Since $b$ is maximal in $B$ and $a \sim b$, $a$ is also maximal in $B$. Therefore: $a \in C(B)$.

By the same logic, since $a$ is maximal in $A$ and $b \sim a$, we have: $b \in C(A)$.

Thus $C$ satisfies HARP. $\blacksquare$

Direction 2: HARP $\implies$ Rationalizable
Starting point: Suppose $C$ satisfies HARP.
Now we do not yet have a preference relation: we construct one from observed choices, and then prove it is rational.

Define the revealed preference relation $\succeq$ by:

$$\boxed{x \succeq y \iff \exists A \ni x, y \text{ such that } x \in C(A)}$$

$x \succeq y$ iff we observe $x$ chosen in at least one menu where $y$ was available.

To prove rationalizability, we verify three sequential properties:

Pick any $x, y \in X$.

Since $C(\{x, y\}) \neq \varnothing$, either:

$$x \in C(\{x, y\}) \quad \text{or} \quad y \in C(\{x, y\}) \quad \text{or both.}$$

Hence:

$$x \succeq y \quad \text{or} \quad y \succeq x$$
$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is complete}}$$

Intuition: Because the choice rule must select at least one winner from every pair, all alternatives are comparable.

Suppose $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$.

Let $A = \{x, y, z\}$. We want to show: $x \succeq z$.

Since $C(A) \neq \varnothing$, at least one of $x, y, z$ belongs to $C(A)$.

Case (a) If $x \in C(A)$:

Then, by definition of revealed preference: $x \succeq z$.

Case (b) If $y \in C(A)$:

Recall that $x \succeq y$. By definition of revealed preference, there exists some set $B$ containing $x, y$ such that $x \in C(B)$.
Since $y \in C(A)$, $x \in C(B)$, and $x, y \in A \cap B$, HARP implies: $x \in C(A)$.
So we return to Case (a). Therefore: $x \succeq z$.

Case (c) If $z \in C(A)$:

Recall that $y \succeq z$. Hence there exists some set $D$ containing $y, z$ such that $y \in C(D)$.
Since $z \in C(A)$, $y \in C(D)$, and $y, z \in A \cap D$, HARP implies: $y \in C(A)$.
This reduces to Case (b), which in turn reduces to Case (a).
Therefore: $x \succeq z$.

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is transitive}}$$
$$z \in C(A) \implies y \in C(A) \implies x \in C(A) \implies x \succeq z$$

We have shown $\succeq$ is rational (complete + transitive). We now show that the maximal elements of $\succeq$ coincide exactly with the observed choices $C(A)$.

First inclusion $C(A) \subseteq C(A, \succeq)$:

Suppose $y \in C(A)$.
Then for every $z \in A$, by definition of revealed preference: $y \succeq z$.
Hence $y$ is maximal in $A$, so $y \in C(A, \succeq)$.
Therefore:

$$\boxed{C(A) \subseteq C(A, \succeq)}$$
Second inclusion $C(A, \succeq) \subseteq C(A)$:

Now suppose $y \in C(A, \succeq)$.
Choose any $w \in C(A)$.
Since $y$ is maximal: $y \succeq w$.
By definition of revealed preference, there exists a menu $B$ containing $y, w$ such that $y \in C(B)$.
Since $w \in C(A)$, $y \in C(B)$, and $y, w \in A \cap B$, HARP implies: $y \in C(A)$.
Therefore:

$$\boxed{C(A, \succeq) \subseteq C(A)}$$
$$\boxed{C(A) = C(A, \succeq)}$$

Therefore, $C$ is rationalizable. $\blacksquare$

PART 3 · EMPIRICAL LIMITS
3. Empirical Limits of Revealed Preference

The characterization theorem ($C \text{ rationalizable} \iff \text{HARP}$) is an exact theoretical equivalence, but applying it to empirical observations exposes major limitations:

Theoretical Requirement
Full correspondence observation

HARP requires knowing the entire choice rule for every subset:

$$C(A) \quad \forall A \subseteq X$$

Requires observing decisions on all possible combinations of goods.

Empirical Reality
Incomplete real-world data
  • Few choice sets: We only observe a finite number of price-income budget sets.
  • Partial choices: We only observe the actual bundle bought, never the full set of tied alternatives.
From HARP to WARP & GARP in Applied Microeconomics:
Because real market datasets are finite and incomplete, empirical revealed preference tests rely on alternative axioms:
  • WARP (Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference): Applied to observed single choices (e.g., individual consumer demand).
  • GARP (Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference): Characterizes utility maximization on finite budget datasets (Afriat’s Theorem), allowing for indifference and flat budget planes.
