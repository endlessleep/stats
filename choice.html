Part I — Choice Theory

3. Choices

Choice functions, rationalizability, Property α, choice correspondences, and HARP.

3.1 Choice Functions

Let $X$ be the set of possible alternatives.

A non-empty subset $A \subseteq X$ from which an agent can choose is called a choice set.

A choice function $C_F$ assigns to every choice set $A$ exactly one alternative:

$$\boxed{C_F(A) \in A}$$
3.2 Rationalizable Choice Functions

A choice function $C_F$ is rationalizable if there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ on $X$ such that, for every choice set $A$, $C_F(A)$ is the unique best alternative in $A$:

$$\boxed{C_F(A) \succ y \qquad \forall y \in A, \ y \neq C_F(A)}$$
Rational
A property of underlying preferences: completeness + transitivity.
Rationalizable
A property of observed choices: they can be generated by rational preferences.

Let $X = \{x, y, h\}$ (e.g. going out with Xavier, with Yoan, or staying home):

$$C(\{x, h\}) = x, \qquad C(\{y, h\}) = y, \qquad C(\{x, y, h\}) = h$$

From $C(\{x, h\}) = x$, we infer $x \succ h$.
However, when $y$ is added to the menu, $h$ is chosen ($C(\{x, y, h\}) = h$) even though $x$ is still available.

Therefore, this choice function is not rationalizable.

3.3 Property α
$$\boxed{\text{A choice function is rationalizable } \iff \text{ it satisfies Property } \alpha.}$$
Definition — Property α
For any $B \subset A \subseteq X$, if $C(A) \in B$, then:
$$\boxed{C(B) = C(A)}$$
If the old winner is still available after some unchosen alternatives are removed, it must remain the winner.

Let $C$ be a rationalizable choice function on $X$.

Then there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ such that, for every choice set $A$, $C(A)$ is the unique best alternative in $A$.

Assume $B \subset A$ and $C(A) \in B$.

Since $C(A)$ is the unique best alternative in $A$:

$$C(A) \succ y \qquad \forall y \in A, \ y \neq C(A)$$

Since $B \subset A$, it is also true that:

$$C(A) \succ y \qquad \forall y \in B, \ y \neq C(A)$$

Hence $C(A)$ is also the unique best alternative in $B$.

Therefore: $C(B) = C(A)$. Thus $C$ satisfies Property $\alpha$. $\blacksquare$

Note: The reverse direction ($\text{Property } \alpha \implies \text{Rationalizable}$) is left as an exercise in the lecture.

3.4 Choice Rules

A choice function chooses one alternative. A choice rule (or choice correspondence) can choose several alternatives:

$$C_R(A) \subseteq A \qquad \text{and} \qquad C_R(A) \neq \varnothing$$

A choice rule is rationalizable if there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ such that:

$$\boxed{C_R(A) = \{x \in A : x \succeq y \quad \forall y \in A\}}$$
  • $C_R(A)$ is the set of maximal alternatives in $A$.
  • Several alternatives can belong to $C_R(A)$ when they are equally preferred.
3.5 Houthakker’s Axiom of Revealed Preferences (HARP)

Given a choice rule $C$, HARP states that for all $a, b \in A \cap B$ such that $a \in C(A)$ and $b \in C(B)$:

$$\boxed{a \in C(B) \quad \text{and} \quad b \in C(A)}$$
Intuition:
— If $a$ is chosen when $b$ is available ($a \in C(A), b \in A$), this reveals $a \succeq b$.
— If $b$ is chosen when $a$ is available ($b \in C(B), a \in B$), this reveals $b \succeq a$.
Therefore $a \sim b$, and both must be maximal in both choice sets.

Note: HARP does not mean $a = b$. It means the observed choices are consistent with mutual revealed preference.

Consider $A = \{a, b, c\}$ with $C(A) = \{a\}$, and $B = \{a, b\}$ with $C(B) = \{b\}$.

  • $a, b \in A \cap B$.
  • $a \in C(A)$ reveals $a \succ b$ (or $a \succeq b$).
  • $b \in C(B)$ reveals $b \succ a$ (or $b \succeq a$).

HARP would require $a \in C(B)$ and $b \in C(A)$, but $a \notin C(B)$ and $b \notin C(A)$.

Conclusion: The choice rule violates HARP and is not rationalizable.

3.6 Main Theorem — Rationalizability and HARP
$$\boxed{\text{Given a finite set } X, \text{ a choice rule is rationalizable } \iff \text{ it satisfies HARP.}}$$
Direction 1: Rationalizable $\implies$ HARP

Suppose $C$ is rationalizable.

Consider $a, b \in A \cap B$ such that $a \in C(A)$ and $b \in C(B)$.

  • By maximality Since $a \in C(A)$ and $b \in A$, we have: $a \succeq b$.
  • By maximality Since $b \in C(B)$ and $a \in B$, we have: $b \succeq a$.

Hence: $a \sim b$.

Since $b$ is maximal in $B$ and $a \sim b$, $a$ is also maximal in $B$.

Therefore: $a \in C(B)$.

Similarly: $b \in C(A)$.

Thus $C$ satisfies HARP. $\blacksquare$

Direction 2: HARP $\implies$ Rationalizable

Suppose $C$ satisfies HARP. Define the revealed preference relation by:

$$\boxed{x \succeq y \iff \exists A \ni x, y \text{ such that } x \in C(A)}$$

$x \succeq y$ iff we have observed $x$ being chosen when $y$ was available.

We now prove that $\succeq$ is complete, transitive, and generates exactly the observed choices:

Pick any $x, y \in X$.

Since $C(\{x, y\}) \neq \varnothing$, either:

$$x \in C(\{x, y\}) \quad \text{or} \quad y \in C(\{x, y\}) \quad \text{or both.}$$

Hence:

$$x \succeq y \quad \text{or} \quad y \succeq x$$
$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is complete}}$$

Intuition: Since the choice rule must choose at least one alternative from every pair, every pair can be ranked.

Suppose $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$.

Let $A = \{x, y, z\}$. We want to show: $x \succeq z$.

Since $C(A) \neq \varnothing$, at least one of $x, y, z$ belongs to $C(A)$.

Case (a) If $x \in C(A)$:

Then, by definition of revealed preference: $x \succeq z$.

Case (b) If $y \in C(A)$:

Recall that $x \succeq y$. Hence, by definition of revealed preference, there exists some set $B$ containing $x, y$ such that $x \in C(B)$.
Since $y \in C(A)$, $x \in C(B)$, and $x, y \in A \cap B$, HARP implies: $x \in C(A)$.
So we are back to Case (a). Therefore: $x \succeq z$.

Case (c) If $z \in C(A)$:

Recall that $y \succeq z$. Hence there exists some set $D$ containing $y, z$ such that $y \in C(D)$.
Since $z \in C(A)$, $y \in C(D)$, and $y, z \in A \cap D$, HARP implies: $y \in C(A)$.
So we are back to Case (b), which brings us to Case (a).
Therefore: $x \succeq z$.

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is transitive}}$$
$$z \in C(A) \implies y \in C(A) \implies x \in C(A) \implies x \succeq z$$

Completeness and transitivity show that the constructed preference relation is rational. We still need to verify that it actually reproduces the observed choice rule.

First inclusion $C(A) \subseteq C(A, \succeq)$:

Suppose $y \in C(A)$.
Then for every $z \in A$, by definition of revealed preference: $y \succeq z$.
Hence $y$ is maximal in $A$, so $y \in C(A, \succeq)$.
Therefore:

$$\boxed{C(A) \subseteq C(A, \succeq)}$$

Intuition: Every observed chosen alternative is maximal under the constructed preferences.

Second inclusion $C(A, \succeq) \subseteq C(A)$:

Now suppose $y \in C(A, \succeq)$.
Choose any $w \in C(A)$.
Since $y$ is maximal: $y \succeq w$.
By definition of revealed preference, there exists a menu $B$ containing $y, w$ such that $y \in C(B)$.
Since $w \in C(A)$, $y \in C(B)$, and $y, w \in A \cap B$, HARP implies: $y \in C(A)$.
Therefore:

$$\boxed{C(A, \succeq) \subseteq C(A)}$$

Intuition: Every maximal alternative predicted by the constructed preferences is actually observed as chosen.

$$\boxed{C(A) = C(A, \succeq)}$$

Therefore, $C$ is rationalizable. $\blacksquare$

$$\boxed{\text{Rationalizable choice rule} \iff \text{HARP}} \quad \text{(for finite } X \text{)}$$
Logical Proof Map:
$$\text{HARP} \implies \text{define revealed preference} \implies \text{complete} \implies \text{transitive} \implies \text{same choices} \implies \text{rationalizable}$$
3.7 Empirical Limits

HARP assumes observation of the full choice correspondence:

$$C(A) \quad \forall A \subset X$$

In practice:

  • Not all possible choices from a given set may be observed.
  • Only some choice sets may be observed.

With finite observed data, other revealed-preference axioms such as WARP and GARP characterize consistency of actual observed choices rather than the entire theoretical choice rule.
